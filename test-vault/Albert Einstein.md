Albert Einstein was a theoretical physicist who is widely held to be one of the greatest and most influential scientists of all time. Best known for developing the theory of relativity, he also made important contributions to quantum mechanics.

His mass–energy equivalence formula, which arises from special relativity, has been called the world's most famous equation. He received the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics for his services to theoretical physics, and especially for his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect.

In 1905, sometimes described as his annus mirabilis, he published four groundbreaking papers. These outlined a theory of the photoelectric effect, explained Brownian motion, introduced his special theory of relativity, and demonstrated that if the special theory is correct, mass and energy are equivalent to each other.

Einstein moved to the United States in 1933 after Adolf Hitler came to power, becoming an American citizen in 1940. On the eve of World War II, he endorsed a letter to President Franklin D. Roosevelt alerting him to the potential German nuclear weapons program.

His intellectual achievements and originality have made the word Einstein broadly synonymous with genius. In a 1999 poll of 130 leading physicists worldwide, Einstein was ranked the greatest physicist of all time.
